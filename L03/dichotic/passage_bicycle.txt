The invention of the modern bicycle took several surprising turns. Early
machines had a single large wheel at the front and a tiny wheel behind, which
made them fast but dangerously unstable. Riders sat almost directly above the
front axle, so a small stone could send them over the handlebars. For nearly
twenty years, engineers tinkered with chains, gears, and rubber tyres, trying to
find a safer arrangement. The breakthrough came when a small chain drive
connected the pedals to the rear wheel, allowing both wheels to be the same
size. This design was cheaper, smoother, and far more comfortable, and within a
decade it had replaced almost every earlier pattern. Manufacturers began
producing thousands of identical frames, and prices fell sharply. For the first
time, ordinary workers could travel several miles to a job in another town. The
bicycle quietly reshaped where people lived, who they married, and how they
spent their weekends, long before the motor car appeared.

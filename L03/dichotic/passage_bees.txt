Honeybees navigate using a remarkable combination of tools. On a clear day they
read the position of the sun, and they can even account for the way it drifts
across the sky over several hours. When clouds hide the sun, they switch to
polarised light, which carries a faint directional signal that human eyes
cannot detect. Bees also remember landmarks, counting and comparing the shapes
of hedges, trees, and buildings along a familiar route. After discovering a
good supply of nectar, a returning forager performs a looping dance on the
vertical comb. The angle of the dance encodes the direction of the food, and
the duration encodes the distance. Other workers crowd around, touch the dancer
with their antennae, and then fly out to the same patch. Experiments with
tiny painted dots have shown that the insects adjust for wind, and that they
prefer the shortest reliable path. Their maps are not pictures, but they are
accurate enough to guide a colony through an entire summer. Move a feeder
overnight and the foragers return to the old place first, search briefly, then
correct their route.
